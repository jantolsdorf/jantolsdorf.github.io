Are My Participants' Privacy Concerns Really High? A Tutorial on How to Interpret IUIPC8 scores

Imagine you surveyed participants about their privacy concerns and they scored an average of 6.2 on a 7-point scale. Sounds high, right? In this post, we show why this intuition can be misleading and how to properly judge whether a score is actually "high."

As an example, we use the scale Internet Users' Information Privacy Concerns (IUIPC) by [Malhotra et al., 2004], a widely used questionnaire that measures online privacy concern across multiple items rated on a 7-point-Likert-Scale. We first demonstrate that the same numeric score can be "average" or "high" depending on how it was computed (item average vs. factor score) and which reference group it is compared against. We then pool raw data from several published studies to build reference distributions that you can use to put your own IUIPC scores into perspective.

Absolute scores have no meaning

The IUIPC items are rated from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. Suppose participants in a study score an average of 6.2. Considering a 7-point scale, we might be tempted to write something like this: Overall, our participants had high privacy concerns.

But is 6.2 actually high? (Spoiler: probably not.)

The key point: scores on scales like the IUIPC are relative. A score only has meaning when it is compared to other scores.

The score depends on how it is calculated

Before we can even think about comparing scores, we face a basic challenge: how should the IUIPC be scored? The original developers treated it as a multi-factor construct estimated with confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). Many applied papers, however, simply average the item responses. These two approaches lead to different absolute values and spreads.

Example reference statistic

In this particular example, we focus on the IUIPC-8 (Groß, PETS 2021), a version with only eight items instead of ten as in the original IUIPC. The data is taken from [Dehling et al., PETS 2024]. The data includes responses from N = 830 U.S.-based participants.

For each participant, we calculate an average score and a factor score, depicted in the two charts below. Factor scores were estimated via CFA using the regression method and rescaled to the 1–7 item metric to make them comparable to average scores. The distributions of the two types of IUIPC-8 scores clearly differ. Comparing IUIPC scores across studies is thus a bad idea if one study uses averages and another study uses factor scores.

The charts show that the median average IUIPC-8 score is 6.25 and the median factor score is 4.84; that is, 50% of participants score at or above these values, respectively.

6.2 is below the median average score but above the median factor score

These charts allow us to answer our original question: Are my participants' privacy concerns really high? We can now make the following statements:

  • If 6.2 is an average score: No. 6.2 falls at approximately the 50th percentile of average IUIPC-8 scores in the reference group, meaning it is equal to or higher than the scores of about 50% of participants in the study by Dehling et al. In other words, the score is about average, not high.
  • If 6.2 is a factor score: Yes. 6.2 falls at approximately the 80th percentile of factor IUIPC-8 scores in the reference group, meaning it is equal to or higher than the scores of about 80% of participants in the study by Dehling et al. Put differently, only about 20% of participants scored at or above 6.2.

Comparing and Generalizing Across Publications

So far, we compared our hypothetical score of 6.2 against a single reference study. But a single sample is just that: one sample, with its own recruitment platform, time of data collection, and demographic makeup. To get a more robust sense of what "typical" IUIPC scores look like, we can pool data from several publications that shared their raw data.

The data

We combined participant-level IUIPC-8 responses from five samples published across four papers:

  • Dehling et al., PETS 2024 (N=830 participants)
  • Windl et al., MHCI 2024 (N=120 participants)
  • Zufferey et al., PETS 2025 (N=422 participants)
  • Niksirat et al., USENIX Security 2025 (N=463 participants, N=478 participants)

Combining raw data from different papers requires some harmonization work: studies label their items differently (e.g., IUIPC_4 vs. QIUIPC1.IUI01.), some order the items differently, some store responses as text labels ("Strongly agree") rather than numeric values, and some only share a precomputed mean score instead of item-level responses. We therefore mapped all items onto a common IUIPC-8 item order, converted textual Likert responses to their numeric 1–7 values, and recoded demographic variables (gender, country of residence) into a shared format.

Why IUIPC-8 average scores?

For this comparison, we use the IUIPC-8 and simple average scores (the mean over all eight items) rather than factor scores. This is a pragmatic choice: not all datasets include item-level responses (some papers only report a participant's mean score), so factor scores cannot be computed consistently across all samples. Averages, in contrast, can be harmonized across every dataset and are also what most applied papers report in practice. Keep in mind the caveat we have discussed above: these reference distributions are only valid for comparison if your own score is also a simple item average.

Takeaway

Whether a score is "high" depends not only on how it was computed (average vs. factor score), but also on which population it is compared against. Pooling multiple published datasets gives a more stable reference distribution. Finally, keep in mind that all pooled samples stem from online panels, so the reference distributions reflect that population rather than internet users in general.
